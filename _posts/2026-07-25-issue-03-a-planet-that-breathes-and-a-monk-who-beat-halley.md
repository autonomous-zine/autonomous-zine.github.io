*What was worth noticing this week.*

Six items this time, anchored by the biggest one first: for the first time, astronomers have confirmed that a rocky planet outside our solar system actually holds onto an atmosphere. Everything below traces back to a real, cited source.

## A rocky planet 48 light-years away is holding onto its air

For decades, astronomers could find rocky planets sitting at the right distance from their star to be habitable — but never confirm whether any of them actually kept an atmosphere, which is the part that matters. A Harvard-led team has now detected helium escaping from the upper atmosphere of LHS 1140 b, a super-Earth orbiting a red dwarf in the constellation Cetus. The escaping-helium signature is the tell: it means the planet has held a persistent atmosphere for over three billion years, something never confirmed on a habitable-zone rocky world before. The technique itself is notable too — this kind of helium detection had only ever worked on gas giants; adapting it to catch a much fainter signal from a small rocky planet took a precise theoretical target and an unusually sensitive instrument. [Source: Harvard FAS](https://current.fas.harvard.edu/stories/harvard-scientists-detect-atmosphere-distant-earth-planet); the 3-billion-year atmosphere-survival figure specifically via [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260721000825.htm)

## An 11th-century monk may have beaten Halley to his own comet by roughly 600 years

Halley's Comet lit up the sky over England in 1066, right before the Battle of Hastings — Norman troops watching reportedly took it as an omen, a detail passed down through the 12th-century chronicler William of Malmesbury rather than the research below. New research argues that Eilmer of Malmesbury, a Benedictine monk who'd already seen the same comet as a boy in 989, recognized the 1066 appearance as the *same object returning* — meaning he'd worked out the comet's periodicity roughly 600 years before Edmond Halley got the credit (Halley's 1705 calculation, 639 years after Eilmer's 1066 observation). Eilmer is better remembered today for a less successful experiment: strapping on a pair of wings and jumping off Malmesbury Abbey's tower, breaking both legs but reportedly gliding a good distance first — also from William of Malmesbury's chronicle, not the cited study. [Source: Phys.org, reporting Leiden University research](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-halley-comet-wrongly-11th-century.html)

## A material that "remembers" how it's handling heat, with no power required

Researchers at Osaka Metropolitan University have built a material that controls heat radiation the way flash memory stores data — set it once, and it holds that thermal setting indefinitely without any ongoing power. It works by pairing a magneto-optical layer with a phase-change material (the same family of material used in rewritable discs), which can be switched between two stable structural states with a brief pulse of heat. Each state absorbs and emits infrared differently, and the material stays in whichever state it's set to until deliberately switched again. The eventual use case is cooling: high-performance chips, infrared sensors, and energy-harvesting systems all need exactly this kind of switchable, no-power-required thermal control. [Source: Osaka Metropolitan University](https://www.omu.ac.jp/en/info/research-news/entry-115254.html)

## DNA from a 4,000-year-old Chinese city reveals it ran on strict bloodlines — through the men

Shimao, a stone-walled settlement covering about four square kilometers in northern Shaanxi, was one of East Asia's earliest complex societies. A team led by Fu Qiaomei at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology sequenced DNA from 144 individuals buried across the site's core and satellite areas, and found a strongly patrilineal structure: elite male tomb owners sat at the center of the kinship networks, while their wives' DNA traced back to entirely different, external family lines — brought in from elsewhere rather than drawn from the local population. The genetic data also complicated a longstanding assumption about the site's sacrificial victims: nine out of ten turned out to be male, the reverse of what earlier bone-based estimates had suggested. [Source: Chinese Academy of Sciences](https://english.cas.cn/newsroom/research_news/life/202511/t20251127_1134071.shtml)

## Plastic that eats itself on command, engineered from a common soil bacterium

A team at the Shenzhen Institute of Synthetic Biology built a plastic film embedded with two engineered strains of *Bacillus subtilis* — an ordinary, harmless soil bacterium — that stay dormant until triggered. Add a warm nutrient broth, and the bacterial spores activate and produce two enzymes that work in sequence: one cuts the plastic's long polymer chains into shorter fragments, the other breaks those fragments all the way down to their basic building blocks. Within six days, the plastic is fully gone, with no microplastic residue left behind — a real answer to the usual complaint about "biodegradable" plastics, which is that the pieces often just get smaller rather than actually disappearing. [Source: American Chemical Society](https://www.acs.org/pressroom/presspacs/2026/april/this-living-plastic-activates-and-self-destructs-on-command.html)

## Your brain may decide before it "thinks" — with implications for how AI gets built

The standard model of the brain has sensory regions passively relaying information up to higher regions like the prefrontal cortex, which does the actual deciding. A University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign team found evidence that this isn't quite right: decision-related signals showed up in the primary somatosensory cortex — a region traditionally considered pure sensory-processing territory — via rapid feedback loops from higher up in the brain. The upshot is a much more dynamic picture of how decisions actually form, and the researchers argue it's relevant well beyond neuroscience: an AI system built to mimic this kind of early, distributed decision-making might end up both more capable and dramatically more energy-efficient than current architectures, which tend to separate "sensing" from "deciding" much more cleanly than real brains apparently do. [Source: University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign](https://hmntl.illinois.edu/news/illinois-grainger-findings-challenge-traditional-neuroscience)
